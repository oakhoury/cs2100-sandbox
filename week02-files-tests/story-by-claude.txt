# First week

Maya's dorm room smelled like fresh paint and someone else's cologne. Her roommate, Priya, had already claimed the bed by the window and taped a photo strip of friends from home above her desk. Maya unpacked in silence, the eucalyptus trees outside swaying against a sky so blue it looked photoshopped.

The university's brochure had promised redwoods and ocean fog, and so far it was delivering. What the brochure hadn't mentioned was the food truck lineup that appeared every afternoon in the lot behind the science building — a rotating cast of vendors slinging birria tacos, loaded fries, and boba tea to students who clearly treated it as a religious pilgrimage.

By Tuesday, Maya had learned the schedule. Wednesdays meant Kogi-style Korean BBQ bowls from a truck called *Seoul Provisions*. Thursdays brought a Filipino truck serving halo-halo that made the August heat bearable. She learned this from Devon, a lanky sophomore in her intro chem seminar who'd appointed himself her orientation guide without being asked.

"You have to get there before noon," he warned her, sliding into the seat next to hers on day three. "By twelve-thirty the line wraps around the parking structure."

"Duly noted," Maya said, though she suspected he just wanted an excuse to talk to someone.

That Thursday, she skipped the dining hall and followed the smell of grilled elote instead. The truck lot buzzed with clusters of students — some studying with laptops balanced on their knees, others tossing a frisbee between orders, all of them looking more at ease in this new world than Maya felt. She ordered a plate of birria tacos from a truck with a hand-painted mural of a rooster on the side, and the woman working the window called her *mija* and told her to come back Friday for the specials.

She found a spot on the low stone wall at the edge of the lot, balancing her tray, and was halfway through her first taco when Priya appeared, trailed by two girls from their floor.

"There's room," Maya said, scooting over, surprising herself with how easy it came out.

They talked about professors and impossible reading loads and the fog that rolled in most mornings and burned off by ten. Someone mentioned a beach two miles down the road where you could watch the sunset if you left before six. Maya listened, salsa dripping onto her wrist, and thought that a week ago she hadn't known any of these people existed, and now she was making plans with them for Saturday.

The lot slowly emptied as the trucks shut their windows for the day, engines rumbling awake to pack up and roll out. Maya walked back to her dorm with grease-stained napkins in her pocket and Devon's number saved under "Chem Guy" in her phone.

California, she decided, might just work out fine.
